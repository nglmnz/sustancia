Sustancia

A project for learning to reason with statistical evidence.
Sustancia · statistical learning laboratory · in development

Reading a table is not the same as interpreting it.

Sustancia is a web-based learning game that helps students interpret statistical results in context, across sociology, psychology, public policy, health, and other fields. The current prototype invites players to write their own interpretations and receive formative feedback. Its next stage explores an initial diagnostic, a multidimensional mastery profile, and activities that respond to students’ learning trajectories.

Statistical reasoning is not a straight climb from a simple answer to a complex one. It brings together knowledge of concepts, statistical operations, interpretation, critical judgment, and the ability to revise an argument. Sustancia explores how to learn across these connected dimensions.

Learning statistics means learning to think with data. Reaching a numerical result is not enough: we must also understand its meaning, question its limitations, and transfer what we have learned.

The difference is who does the intellectual work

AI can deliver a finished answer or help someone revise their own interpretation. Sustancia proposes the second path: produce, receive feedback, and think again.

Statistical reasoning has more than one dimension

Statistical reasoning is not a single skill, and learning it is not a universal linear path. It involves understanding concepts, selecting and applying procedures, interpreting evidence in context, examining uncertainty and assumptions, and revising arguments. A learner may be strong in one area while still developing another.

N1–N4 describes interpretive depth—only one dimension of the proposed mastery profile. It is a useful lens for comparing interpretations of a particular output, not a four-step sequence through which all statistical learning must pass.

A learner may get the calculation right but struggle to interpret it. Someone else may reason critically yet need more practice with procedures. Sustancia’s proposed mastery profile therefore separates concepts, operations, interpretive depth, recurring errors, and development over time, rather than reducing learning to one grade.

See the difference

Consider one dimension of this broader model: interpretive depth. The two responses below use the same illustrative regression table. Their comparison shows different ways of interpreting a result; it does not define a mandatory N1 → N4 path or capture the entirety of statistical reasoning.

Illustrative regression example for teaching purposes; figures are not presented as verified estimates from the study.
Predictor β p
Compulsive use (IDA) .38 < .001
Gender (woman) .24 .002
Screen time .05 .41
LEVEL 1 · TECHNICAL READING

“Compulsive use and gender are statistically significant (p < .05). Screen time is not statistically significant.”

Technical reading: identifies the reported statistical significance without yet discussing the substantive question.
LEVEL 3 · SUBSTANTIVE INTERPRETATION

“In this illustrative model, compulsive use shows a positive association with poorer mental health, while screen time has no statistically significant coefficient. This raises questions about patterns of use as well as duration, without proving causality or showing that screen time has no effect.”

Substantive interpretation: relates the estimates to a research question and states what the evidence cannot establish.

What this comparison does—and does not—show: N1 and N3 are examples of interpretive depth, not ranks of the learner as a whole. A non-significant p-value is not evidence of zero effect, and an association by itself does not establish causality. Different statistical tasks mobilize other dimensions of the mastery profile.

How it is intended to work

The adaptive design envisages an initial diagnostic, a multidimensional mastery profile, curriculum-linked activity selection, and a guided revision cycle. Students begin by taking on challenges provided by the system; they do not upload documents. Course materials and exercises are reviewed by the academic team.

The intellectual foundation

The project takes inspiration from Gilbert Simondon: learning statistics means more than operating techniques. It involves understanding the decisions that produce results, the assumptions behind them, and the conclusions they cannot support.

The prototype’s original material comes from an empirical study of technological habitus and mental health among first-year university students (n = 276). The broader educational proposal now extends to statistical learning across disciplines.
